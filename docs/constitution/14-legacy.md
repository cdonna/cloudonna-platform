# Legacy

*If ClouDonna, the company, no longer exists in fifty years — acquired, dissolved, or simply outcompeted — what should still be true because it once did?*

Not its product. Products are instruments, and every instrument is eventually replaced by a better one; wanting otherwise would contradict this constitution's own belief that even the company's tools are temporary. What should outlast the company is a set of standards the world came to expect, that no later company found through the same catalog of hard, un-cinematic decisions this one made.

**That a computed answer and a narrated answer are never allowed to look the same.** If the distinction between judgment and computation becomes a basic, assumed feature of any serious advisory system built after this company existed — not because a regulation demanded it, but because customers came to expect it and would distrust anything that blurred it — that expectation is this company's legacy, independent of whether the company itself is still the one supplying it.

**That a business claiming neutrality can be structurally checked, not just trusted on its word.** If "show me the mechanism, not just the promise" becomes the normal question a buyer asks any system claiming to be unbiased, that habit of mind is worth more than any single company that helped install it.

**That a permanent record of judgment is something an organization is expected to have, not a luxury only the largest ones can afford.** If mid-sized enterprises come to assume they should be able to answer "why did we decide this" years later, the way they already assume they should be able to answer "what did we spend," the practice mattered more than the company that normalized it.

**That outcomes are allowed to inform judgment without being allowed to secretly override it.** If the norm survives that a system's memory can teach without being permitted to silently rewrite its own rules, this company will have contributed a real boundary to a debate that will still be happening long after any particular product built around it is gone.

**That "we don't know yet" remained an acceptable, respected answer from a system built to advise.** Of everything on this list, this is the one most vulnerable to erosion under commercial pressure, and the one most worth having actually held, even briefly, in a world that increasingly rewarded whoever sounded most certain.

## What legacy is not, on this list

Market share. A valuation. Being first. None of these survive the company that achieved them in any way that matters to anyone who wasn't an investor in it. What's listed above is different in kind: each one is a standard the world could plausibly hold itself to *after* forgetting which company first insisted on it. That kind of forgetting is not a failure of this legacy. It is what legacy actually looks like when it succeeds — the origin becomes irrelevant because the standard became normal.
