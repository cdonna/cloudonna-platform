# A Letter to the First Employee

*Written by Marvin, for whoever joins first. Not a motivational document — an honest one.*

I'm not going to tell you this will be easy, or that we're going to change the world, or any of the other things founders say in letters like this because they're expected to. I'm going to tell you what you're actually signing up for, so that if you say yes, you're saying yes to the real thing.

We are building a company whose entire value depends on people believing we are telling them the truth, even when the truth is less convenient than a confident guess would be. That is a harder business than it sounds. Every day, there will be a faster way to make a demo more impressive, a smoother way to answer a hard question, a shortcut that would close a deal this quarter instead of next year. Almost none of those shortcuts will look like a betrayal of anything when they're in front of you. They will look like reasonable, practical, everyone-does-this decisions. Our job is to recognize them anyway, and to say no anyway, even when no one but us would ever know the difference.

Here is what that means in practice. When we don't know something, we say we don't know it — to a customer, to an investor, to each other — even when a vaguer answer would sound better in the room. When our own product isn't ready yet, we say it isn't ready, not "coming soon" dressed up to sound like it already works. When someone offers us money in exchange for influence over what we recommend, the answer is no, regardless of how the budget looks that month. And when we get something wrong — and we will, more than once — we say so plainly, fix the actual thing that was wrong, and don't spend more energy managing how it looks than we spent understanding what happened.

I want you to understand the responsibility that comes with this specifically, not in the abstract. People are going to make decisions worth more money than either of us will make in our careers, informed in part by what we build. Some of those decisions will go well because of good evidence we gave them. Some will go badly despite it, because that's what happens when real decisions are made under real uncertainty. Our job is not to guarantee good outcomes — nobody can promise that honestly. Our job is to make sure the reasoning behind every one of those decisions was sound, evidenced, and ours to stand behind, regardless of how it turned out. If you're not comfortable with that weight, this isn't the place for you, and I'd rather know that now than after you've built something you're proud of, on a foundation you don't actually believe in.

What must never be sacrificed: the independence of what we tell people, the honesty of what we admit we don't know, and the permanence of what we said we did, once we've said it. Everything else — the product, the market, even the company's name — can change. Those three things are the actual company. If we're ever in a room deciding whether to trade one of them for growth, revenue, or convenience, I need you to say so out loud, the same way I'm asking you to trust that I will.

Thank you for being the first person who believed this was worth building before there was any proof it would work. That's the only kind of trust this letter can actually ask for.

— Marvin
