# The Definition of Quality

## Why accuracy alone is the wrong test

An answer can turn out to be right by luck, arrived at through reasoning that happened to survive contact with reality this one time and would not survive it again. An answer can turn out to be wrong despite being the most defensible conclusion available at the time, undone by something no reasonable process could have foreseen. Judging quality by outcome alone rewards the first case and punishes the second, which teaches exactly the wrong lesson to anyone paying attention: that being convincing matters more than being sound. A definition of quality built only on "was it right" will, over enough decisions, optimize for looking right rather than being reasoned well — the two are not the same thing, and only one of them is worth building a company around.

## What a high-quality recommendation actually is

A recommendation the decision-maker could not have easily produced alone, built through a process a skeptical outsider could inspect and find sound, that states its own uncertainty honestly rather than smoothing it away, and that remains defensible in hindsight *regardless of how the outcome turned out* — because a sound decision made under real uncertainty can still have a bad outcome, and an unsound one can still get lucky. Quality lives in the integrity of the reasoning process, not in whether the world later cooperated with it.

## The four tests

**Was the reasoning inspectable?** A recommendation whose logic cannot be traced by someone with no stake in the answer is not high quality, however confident it sounds — inspectability is not a nice-to-have layered on top of the answer, it is part of what makes the answer an answer rather than an assertion.

**Was the uncertainty honest?** A recommendation that overstates its own confidence has failed regardless of whether it happened to be right — the failure is in the claim, not the outcome, and a claim can be dishonest while still getting lucky.

**Was it something the decision-maker could act on?** An impeccably evidenced conclusion that offers no path to a decision is not high quality, it's an academic exercise wearing the clothes of advice. Quality requires being useful to the actual moment of choice, not just correct in the abstract.

**Would it have been defensible the day it was made, independent of what happened after?** This is the test that separates quality from luck: ask whether the recommendation was the right call *given only what was knowable at the time*, not given what became knowable afterward. A good decision that went badly is still a good decision. A bad decision that went well is still a bad decision, and pretending otherwise teaches everyone downstream to stop reasoning carefully.

## The uncomfortable consequence of this definition

It means a recommendation can be simultaneously high quality and wrong, and simultaneously low quality and right. Most institutions are not built to hold both of those ideas at once, because it's more comfortable to grade on outcomes alone. This company is built specifically to hold both, because the alternative — grading quality by hindsight — is exactly the dishonesty this entire enterprise exists to be the corrective to.
