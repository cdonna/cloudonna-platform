# The Founder Compass

A permanent test, meant to outlast any specific process built to apply it. Before any initiative — a feature, a partnership, a hire, a market, a pricing model — ask these six questions, in order. Stop at the first no.

1. **Does it increase trust?**
2. **Does it improve decision quality?**
3. **Does it strengthen independence?**
4. **Does it compound learning?**
5. **Does it simplify complexity?**
6. **Does it reinforce our category?**

## If any answer is no

**Why are we doing it?**

That question is not rhetorical. It has exactly three honest answers, and only one of them is acceptable:

- *"We're not — you're right, we shouldn't."* The correct outcome most of the time this compass is actually consulted honestly.
- *"We need the money to survive long enough to build the things that would answer yes."* Sometimes true, and worth saying out loud precisely because it's dangerous — this is the exact reasoning that justifies every principle a company has ever quietly abandoned. If this is the honest answer, it must be treated as a survival exception, timestamped, and revisited the moment survival is no longer the reason, never allowed to quietly become the new normal.
- *"We were wrong about the compass, not the initiative."* Rare, but possible — occasionally reveals the compass itself needs to be reexamined rather than the proposal rejected. This should be treated with suspicion, not comfort: it is also exactly what someone would say to avoid an uncomfortable no.

Any other answer — "everyone else does this," "it'll be fine," "we'll fix it later," "the customer won't notice" — is not an answer. It's the moment the compass was consulted and then ignored, and it should be named as exactly that, not dressed up as a legitimate fourth category.

## Why this compass, and not a longer one

Six questions is few enough to actually ask, every time, out loud, without it becoming a formality nobody really does. A compass with twenty dimensions gets applied to the first three decisions and then quietly stops being used, because no one has the patience to run the whole list under real time pressure — and a compass that stops being used is worse than no compass at all, because it creates the appearance of discipline without the substance of it.

## What this compass is for, stated plainly

Not to make every decision easy. To make sure that when a decision is made against the company's own better judgment — which will happen, because companies are run by people under real pressure — it is made *knowingly*, with the trade-off named, instead of happening quietly enough that no one had to admit what was actually being traded away.
