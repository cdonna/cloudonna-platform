# Principles

Twelve. Not a starting list — the ceiling. Each one is stated once, tested against a real behavior, so it can be checked rather than merely admired.

## 1. Judgment and computation are distinct, and must remain visibly distinct

**Meaning:** a conclusion that was calculated and a conclusion that was narrated are different things, and the difference must always be legible to the person relying on either.
**Why it exists:** the moment the two blur, no one — including the company — can say anymore which part of an answer is reproducible and which part is persuasive.
**Correct behavior:** label which parts of an output were computed and which were explained, every time, even when it's less elegant to show the seam.
**Incorrect behavior:** presenting a narrated explanation as if it were itself the source of the answer.
**Example:** an explanation may say why a number is what it is; it may never quietly become the reason the number changes.

## 2. Silence about a limitation is a form of dishonesty

**Meaning:** not saying something false is not the same as saying everything true that matters.
**Why it exists:** an omission chosen because the truth is inconvenient has the same effect on the listener as a lie, whatever the intent behind it was.
**Correct behavior:** state a real limitation before being asked about it.
**Incorrect behavior:** answering only the question that was asked when a more important, unasked question is the one that actually matters.
**Example:** if a claim rests on old or thin evidence, that is said plainly, not left to be discovered later.

## 3. Confidence must always travel with what produced it

**Meaning:** a number without its basis is not information, it's a performance of information.
**Why it exists:** a reader cannot judge what they cannot see the reasoning behind.
**Correct behavior:** show the decomposition, not just the summary.
**Incorrect behavior:** presenting a single score as if precision were the same thing as certainty.
**Example:** "high confidence, because these four things are true" is acceptable; "92% confidence" alone is not.

## 4. What is easy to automate is not always what should be automated

**Meaning:** capability is not the same as permission.
**Why it exists:** the fact that a step *can* be removed from human judgment says nothing about whether it *should* be.
**Correct behavior:** ask who loses accountability if this step is automated, before asking how to automate it.
**Incorrect behavior:** automating a step because a competitor did, or because it demos well.
**Example:** narrating a recommendation faster is a good use of automation; deciding it is not, no matter how fast the alternative would be.

## 5. Growth that requires quiet compromise is not growth, it's borrowing

**Meaning:** revenue or scale obtained by relaxing a principle is a loan against the company's future credibility, not an asset.
**Why it exists:** the bill for a quietly compromised principle always comes due, usually at the worst possible moment and to someone other than the person who made the trade.
**Correct behavior:** name the trade-off out loud before making it, and decline it if it touches a non-negotiable.
**Incorrect behavior:** letting a compromise happen gradually enough that no single moment felt like the decision.
**Example:** turning down a deal that requires a hidden exception is the correct outcome, however painful in the moment.

## 6. A tool may inform an irreversible act; it must never perform one

**Meaning:** the line between advising and acting is absolute, regardless of how trustworthy the advice has become.
**Why it exists:** trust in advice and authority to act are different grants, and conflating them removes the human check that makes either one safe.
**Correct behavior:** require an explicit human action at every irreversible step, permanently, without exception for convenience.
**Incorrect behavior:** treating a long track record of good advice as a reason to eventually skip the human step.
**Example:** a system that has been right a thousand times in a row still does not get to act on the thousand-and-first without a person choosing to let it.

## 7. The hardest customer question deserves the most complete answer, not the safest one

**Meaning:** difficulty is a signal to go deeper, not a signal to deflect.
**Why it exists:** a company that gets vague exactly when the stakes get high has revealed what its honesty was actually worth.
**Correct behavior:** answer the uncomfortable question as rigorously as the easy one.
**Incorrect behavior:** retreating into caveats or generalities specifically when a direct answer would be unflattering.
**Example:** "here is exactly where our evidence is weakest" is a harder sentence to say than most marketing copy, and the correct one to say anyway.

## 8. Every institution the company depends upon for a capability must be replaceable without notice to the customer

**Meaning:** no external dependency — a technology, a partner, a data source — may become load-bearing enough that its failure becomes the customer's problem.
**Why it exists:** the customer's trust is in the company, not in whichever supplier the company currently happens to rely on.
**Correct behavior:** design every dependency behind an interface that could be swapped without the customer ever knowing it happened.
**Incorrect behavior:** building a capability that only works because of one specific vendor's specific behavior.
**Example:** whatever generates explanatory language today should be replaceable by whatever generates it better tomorrow, invisibly.

## 9. Complexity is the company's burden to absorb, never the customer's to decode

**Meaning:** real difficulty in the underlying problem does not excuse a confusing answer.
**Why it exists:** a customer who has to work to understand a recommendation will trust it less than the evidence actually warrants, which defeats the purpose of gathering the evidence at all.
**Correct behavior:** do the hard work of simplifying the presentation, however hard the underlying reasoning was to produce.
**Incorrect behavior:** exposing raw complexity and calling it rigor.
**Example:** ten dimensions of evidence, shown as one clear structure, not ten separate burdens placed on the reader.

## 10. Disagreement handled honestly is a sign of health, not a threat to resolve quickly

**Meaning:** two honest conclusions that conflict is useful information, not an embarrassment to paper over.
**Why it exists:** false consensus is more dangerous than visible disagreement, because it hides the uncertainty instead of surfacing it.
**Correct behavior:** show a genuine contradiction plainly, and say that it hasn't been resolved yet if it hasn't.
**Incorrect behavior:** quietly picking one side of a real disagreement and presenting it as settled.
**Example:** two sources that disagree about the same fact are shown as disagreeing, not silently averaged.

## 11. What was learned belongs first to the person who lived it

**Meaning:** an institution does not have the right to generalize someone's specific, hard-won experience into a broader lesson without their knowledge and consent.
**Why it exists:** learning at scale is only legitimate if it doesn't quietly strip ownership from the people who generated the underlying experience.
**Correct behavior:** ask before abstracting someone's specific situation into a pattern others will benefit from.
**Incorrect behavior:** treating every interaction as free raw material for the company's own improvement by default.
**Example:** a customer's specific, identifying details never travel into a shared lesson without their explicit agreement — the pattern can travel; the person's story does not, unless they choose to let it.

## 12. The company must be willing to explain any decision about itself as plainly as it asks its product to explain any decision about a customer

**Meaning:** the standard the company holds its product to is the standard the company holds itself to, with no separate, more forgiving version reserved for internal use.
**Why it exists:** a company that demands rigor from its product but not from its own choices does not actually believe in the rigor — it's selling a standard it doesn't live by.
**Correct behavior:** be able to state, honestly and specifically, why any consequential decision about the company was made, to anyone who asks, at any time.
**Incorrect behavior:** having a public answer and a private, truer one that would not survive being said out loud.
**Example:** if the product's rule is "no unsupported claim ships," the company's own claims about itself are held to the identical rule.
