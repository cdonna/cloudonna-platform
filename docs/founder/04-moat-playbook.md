# Moat Playbook

Full analysis: `docs/company/04-decision-intelligence-moat.md`, `16-founder-questions.md` Q6–7. This document is the decisive version — every candidate moat gets a verdict, not just a ranking, because a playbook that only ranks things still lets a weak moat absorb real investment by sitting respectably in the middle of a list.

## Rejected outright — do not invest here believing it is a moat

- **UI/design quality.** Real, valuable, table stakes for winning deals — and the fastest thing on this entire list for a well-funded competitor to match. Investment here is justified for winning the current deal in front of you, never justified as a long-term defensibility bet.
- **The specific deterministic scoring formula.** Genuinely well-built, and copyable by anyone who studies enough output. Protect it as good engineering; never present it internally or externally as the moat.
- **Prompt engineering, provider selection.** By design (`01-founder-principles.md` #6), this cannot be a moat — treating it as one would contradict the company's own founding architecture.
- **The current vendor catalog.** Explicitly illustrative, not proprietary, disclosed as such in the product itself. Zero moat value until it's replaced with real, verified evidence — at which point it graduates to a different item on this list entirely, not an improved version of this one.

## Medium — real, worth building, not sufficient alone

- **The deterministic-authority architecture itself** (AI narrates, never decides). Hard to copy only because it requires a competitor to *forgo* a faster, cheaper path most won't choose under their own investor pressure — a behavioral moat, not a technical one. Durable as long as the discipline holds; worthless the moment it's treated as optional.
- **Structural vendor-neutrality.** Same shape of moat — commercially uncomfortable to hold, which is exactly what makes it real when held and worthless the instant a competitor decides it's negotiable.
- **The explainability layer** (evidence trace, sensitivity analysis, confidence decomposition). Genuinely engineering-intensive; eventually replicable by a well-resourced, patient competitor.

## Strong — requires real time and real customers; capital cannot shortcut it

- **Real customer decision history**, once it exists. Three years of one enterprise's actual decisions is not purchasable by a competitor who started later, at any price.
- **Evidence graph corroboration depth**, once built on real (not illustrative) facts.
- **Enterprise trust and category ownership** (`03-category-playbook.md`). Slow, asymmetric, real.

## Compounding — the only tier worth organizing the company's long-term attention around

- **Outcome-linked decisions.** Ranked #1 in every version of this analysis so far, for the same reason every time: it requires real customers, real elapsed time, and real honesty about what happened — nothing a competitor's engineering budget can accelerate.
- **The decision-quality feedback loop**, once System of Learning exists and the "never silently retrain scoring" principle is exercised long enough to be proven, not just promised.

## The verdict, stated as an allocation rule

**Nothing on the Rejected list gets protected with secrecy, patents, or defensive engineering effort — it gets built well because quality wins deals, and left at that.** Founder and engineering attention this year belongs almost entirely to the *precondition* for the Strong and Compounding tiers (real evidence, real customers, real elapsed time), not to further polishing anything in the Medium tier past "good enough to be credible." A team that spends its scarce early attention perfecting a Medium-tier moat has, whether or not anyone said it out loud, decided that's the company's actual moat — which this document exists specifically to prevent by accident.

## The one uncomfortable implication worth stating directly

Because Strong and Compounding moats both require real elapsed time that cannot be bought, **the single highest-leverage moat-building action available this year is not an engineering decision at all — it's getting the clock started on real customer usage as early as honestly possible.** Every week without a real design partner is a week the company's *only* truly compounding assets are not accumulating, regardless of how much roadmap work happens in the meantime.
